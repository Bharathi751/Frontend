01 — A century of lost velocity

The counterfactual is not a world without computers. Computers could still exist in laboratories, factories and government offices. What disappears is the enormous service economy built around software: enterprise applications, internet platforms, cloud services, IT outsourcing, digital payments, software exports and the global engineering labor market.

That distinction matters. The world would not freeze. It would become more expensive to coordinate. More work would remain inside institutions, on paper or in specialized hardware, because the software layer that makes information portable would be weaker.

02 — Global stagnation would be uneven

Finance would likely remain slower because transaction processing, fraud detection, risk analysis and global market connectivity would be less automated. Supply chains would carry more inventory because firms would have less real-time visibility. Airlines and shipping companies would still operate, but scheduling and optimization would be more cumbersome.

Science would be especially different. Modern research depends on distributed data, computational tools, shared repositories, digital publishing and global collaboration. Without the IT revolution, scientific progress might continue — but the network effects would be weaker.

“The biggest missing invention would not be the computer. It would be cheap, global coordination.

03 — Chennai before the code

Chennai entered the software era with a serious industrial base already in place: automobiles, engineering, ports, manufacturing, healthcare and education. The IT boom did not create the city's industrial identity from nothing. It added a new export engine and then reshaped urban geography around it.

TIDEL Park and the OMR corridor became symbols of this shift. In the counterfactual, those parcels of land and transport corridors do not disappear. Their economic function changes.
